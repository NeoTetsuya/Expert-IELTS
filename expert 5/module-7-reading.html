Letchworth Garden City

The development of the world's first garden city

The idea of a 'garden city' was first proposed by Ebenezer Howard in the late 1800s. He wanted to build new towns that would be totally different from English cities of that time, where overcrowding, dirt, unemployment and poverty were very common. Howard had a dream of cities where the residents would live in harmony with nature. He wanted to give ordinary people a better place to live.

In 1902, Howard wrote a book that was very popular and important in promoting the idea of garden cities. The book, Garden Cities of To-Morrow, set out his ideas for what the new communities should look like. Each area of the city needed to be carefully planned. For example, areas of housing needed to be of a similar size to industrial zones, but in a different place. Importantly, Howard wanted countryside with areas of trees and fields right around the city. This was to prevent the city from spreading and becoming too big. The term greenbelt was used for this area of countryside, and when the city grew in size and reached this point, Howard suggested a new garden city should be built nearby.

A competition was announced in 1903 which would allow architects to develop the best design for a city that matched Howard's dreams. In September of that year, Barry Parker and Raymond Unwin were appointed as architects and the First Garden City company was established. The organisation bought a large area of land about 50 kilometres north of London and the building of Letchworth soon began.

In 1905, First Garden City held a show called The Cheap Cottages Exhibition. The purpose of this exhibition was for designers to try to create housing that was cheaper to build and to show it to the thousands of visitors who came to see the displays. Ideas that were new at that time were for homes to have front and back gardens, for buildings to come pre-made and easy to put together, and for structures to be made from new materials that were cheaper to use, such as concrete instead of bricks.

Howard's plans for Letchworth made good use of circular patterns. This meant that he didn't want a confused mix of roads and streets going in all directions and leading to different areas. He wanted to create a pattern in Letchworth where a few main roads led into the centre of the city, and several minor roads circled around the centre. As a result of this, in 1909 Letchworth became the birthplace of the first roundabout – the best design to allow several roads to meet and form a circle, allowing drivers to easily change direction.

The new city soon attracted businesses. One of the most important manufacturers to arrive in Letchworth was the clothing company Spirella. They began building a large factory in 1912 close to the middle of town. Because it was built to look like a large country house, it managed to blend in* with the other buildings around it.

In 1913, Ebenezer Howard founded the Garden Cities and Town Planning Association. His goal in doing this was to provide a way for town-planners and people concerned about the environment to meet together and share their knowledge. He believed this was the way forward for a better way of life for all people.

Since Letchworth was first built, several housing estates* have been added on to it. One of them is called Jackmans Estate. It was built with its own schools, shops, library and community centre. The plan of the estate was impressive and it is still considered to be a good example of urban design. One success of the plan was to separate pedestrians* from motor traffic, so most homes were built so that they opened onto pedestrian squares, gardens and playgrounds. However, in some cases the actual housing itself varied in quality. Because different construction methods were tried for the first time when Jackmans Estate was built, it became clear that some of these methods were not as good as more traditional methods. Furthermore, over time, people's way of life changed and although the small street of shops at Jackmans Estate does very well, it has lost the local primary school and library.

Glossary
* blend in:
if something blends in with objects, it is similar to them and you do not notice it
* housing estate:
a large number of houses built together in a planned way
* pedestrian:
someone who is walking, especially along a street or other place used by cars
