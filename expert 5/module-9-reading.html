The rise of robot journalists?

A Experts are increasingly sure that computers will soon be able to do many human jobs in less time and for less money. The idea that machines are taking human jobs is not a new one; it dates back to the Industrial Revolution, when the production of cotton rapidly increased due to the development of machines that could weave the material much faster. Today, however, the process of technological change is speeding up. Robots can make cars, play chess, pick fruit and stack shelves. Some health companies claim their robots can even diagnose disease better than doctors. Academics at Oxford University warn that within the next 20 years machines are likely to do 47 percent of jobs in America.

B So should we be worried? The truth is that robots have certain strengths and certain weaknesses. They are very good at tasks that require repetition and once they have learned to stack a shelf or navigate a car, they will never lose the ability. More than that, unlike humans, there is never any sickness, which is one of the reasons some companies are investing a lot of money into researching robotics. The dream of many industries is 'the dark factory' - one in which you do not even need to turn on the lights because there aren't any humans to require them. But what are robots not so good at? How do they perform in more creative industries such as journalism? It may be hard to believe, but it's true: robots are entering journalism, producing simple stories that follow a formula such as financial reports, earthquake alerts or even summaries of football matches.

C Recently, journalist Sarah O'Connor tested the skills of a robot called Emma - a robot programmed to deliver professional services such as research and analysis. Both Sarah and Emma had to write a short article about employment figures in the UK. The test was to see who could write the better article and who could write fastest - the human or robot journalist? According to O'Connor, Emma was fast (12 minutes, compared to her 35 minutes), but this wasn't the most important thing. What Sarah noticed was that the quality of Emma's writing was better than she expected. Her facts were right and she even included information about wider political issues that affect unemployment rates. But Emma lacked the most important journalistic skill of all: the ability to decide what information would be interesting for readers and what would be just plain boring.

D In contrast to this, UK politician David Willetts believes it will be professionals such as journalists who will see robots taking over their jobs. He argues that straightforward manual tasks such as making a cup of tea are actually much harder for robots to imitate. Jobs requiring high level thinking skills, such as creating news stories, are much easier for computer programmers to copy. Although journalism technology is currently limited to computer generated financial reports, Willetts is confident robots will soon be able to create more advanced texts.

E Author Robert Colville is more optimistic about the future. He admits that robots are good at routine tasks, but questions whether they will be able to provide a 'human touch' any time soon. While a robo-doctor might be able to measure our heart rate, it cannot tell whether we may actually be visiting for other reasons that are not physical, such as some unpleasant family news. For Colville, as soon as a job becomes complex or creative - or involves dealing with humans rather than procedures - the robots stop being as useful. They can talk us through a script at the call centre, but they cannot come to our bedside and give us a drink of water and some words of comfort. Are journalists safe then? According to Colville, yes. In a future with robots, it is better to be in a job connected to the public rather than systems and routine.

F Robbie Allen is the CEO of a computer company that generates short articles with a regular pattern, such as sporting or company results. He believes journalists' jobs are safe - his robo-journalists are not trying to compete with them they are providing a different kind of service and a different kind of story. His computers produce hundreds of millions of sports and financial news reports every year. These are rates and levels of production that have never been known before in the history of journalism.

G News industry analyst Ken Doctor sees the relationship between human and robot journalists as more like colleagues. Doctor argues that the role of the journalist is changing; it is becoming more highly skilled. As a result, robots will act as assistants preparing paragraphs full of facts and data. The human journalist will then take these paragraphs and add analysis, interpretation and experience. The robot 'research assistants' may even lead to better journalism as stories will be more accurate. They may even make journalists' jobs easier and more fun.
