Is it better to give or to receive?

It is commonly said that giving is good for us. Now some research has provided clear evidence of this. One organisation promoting the benefits of giving is Action for Happiness, which presents doing things for others as the first item on the list of things that can make us healthier and happier. It’s not just about giving money to charity, we can give time, ideas and energy. Giving could be a regular activity, such as volunteering, or it could be expressed through acts of kindness to friends, neighbours or even strangers.

Various studies have found that people who do acts of kindness, or spend money on others rather than themselves, experience increased happiness and well-being. One study even found there was such a thing as ‘helper’s high’. It seems that when we give money to good causes, it activates the same areas of the brain as if we were receiving money or enjoying food.

So far so good. But there are pitfalls. There is a question of motivation. These days, if I receive a compliment, it crosses my mind that the person may be saying something pleasant largely because they want to increase their own sense of being a nice person. But does this matter? According to one view, it might not be a real problem as kind words and acts are likely to have a positive impact on others, whatever the original motive.

People also need to be careful about giving too much. There are some people who always seem to be giving, perhaps too often. Is there a reason for this that they should be aware of, and is it good for them? Then it is a fact that receiving is not entirely unproblematic either. For all sorts of reasons, doubtless connected to complicated thoughts and feelings about themselves, some people are uncomfortable about receiving. That’s definitely something to think about as rejecting the kindness of others could interfere with friendships and relationships. Receiving gratefully is, after all, a way of making it easier for others to give.

Asking whether it is better to give or to receive might seem to be a question about whether it is better to be focused on oneself or on others. Yet, to ask the question in the first place is to assume that our main concern is what is better for us rather than for others. Most value systems put forward the win-win answer that our own well-being is best served by pro-social behaviour. A cynic would say that this is simply a good way to keep people under control. But there is a good deal of truth in the idea that we all gain from cooperation. Nor does the simple fact that goodness tends to be of advantage to the giver prove that motivations are always selfish: kindness can still be sincere, even if it is repaid with interest.

I think we should think more deeply about this and ask what ‘best for ourselves’ really means. If it is simply feeling good, living long and enjoying pleasures, that seems a shallow reward for moral living. But we could take a more demanding view of what is in our own best interests, believing that there is a real value in living a good life, a value that is not simply paid for in terms of a feel-good effect. From this point of view, giving is good because no matter how much pleasure we might gain from taking, if that is all we do, we could not look at our own lives and in all honesty judge them to be good. That would be a loss of meaning, value and self-respect far more significant than any losses of narrower self-interest.
