Can your musical tastes offer a window into how you think?

Do you like your jazz to be Norah Jones or Ornette Coleman, your classical music to be Bach or Stravinsky, or your rock to be Coldplay or Faith No More? The answer could give a better understanding of the way you think, say researchers from the University of Cambridge. We know that our music choices fluctuate over time as we grow and develop. For example, when people are very young, they may feel pressured into associating with a particular kind of music. And then we all choose some songs on the basis of our emotions at any one moment. However, these things are fairly temporary and often pass. Researchers at Cambridge believe that a person’s taste in musical genre can be reliably predicted from cognitive factors, such as the way they view others, or the way they approach problem-solving.

Music is an important feature of everyday life and it is nearly everywhere we go. For this reason, it has attracted a lot of attention from psychologists. Previous studies have analysed how certain songs make us feel. Historical studies have looked at the way music is used culturally, such as in ceremonies to unite people. However, little was known about what determines our overall taste in music until this study research was published.

In the study of musical tastes, a team of psychologists identified two groups of people: ‘Empathizers’ who like to focus on and respond to the emotions of others, and ‘systemizers’ who like to analyse rules and patterns in the world. They found that by categorizing people into these two groups, they could more accurately predict what type of music they liked.

The researchers conducted a long and ambitious project, with multiple studies of over 4,000 participants who were found mainly through social media sites. They asked social media users to take a selection of psychology-based questionnaires; then the researchers placed the results on participants’ social media profiles for others to see. At a later date, the same people were asked to listen to and rate fifty musical pieces. The researchers used library examples of music from twenty-six genres and subgenres with all of the volunteers to minimize the chances that participants would have any personal or national associations with the piece of music.

What they found was unexpected. According to David Greenberg from the Department of Psychology, although people’s music choices change over time, this study shows that a person’s ability to relate to others and their thinking style predicts what kind of music they like. He went on to argue that their cognitive style – whether they are strong on empathy or strong on systems – and how they communicate with others can be a better predictor of what music they like than their personality. For example, people who scored high on empathy tended to prefer soft music (from R&B, soft rock), unpretentious music (from country, folk) and contemporary music (from electronica, Latin, acid jazz, and Euro pop). They disliked intense music, such as punk and heavy metal. In contrast, people who scored high on systemizing favoured intense music. The results were the same even within a particular musical genre such as jazz: Empathizers preferred chill-out jazz, while systemizers preferred more adventurous, sophisticated jazz.

The researchers also analysed the music itself, and found that those who scored high on sociability preferred music that had low energy, and was often used to express negative emotions, such as feeling lovesick or depressed. They also preferred more emotional depth in their music. Those who were more systematic in tests preferred music that had high energy or positive emotions, and showed a high level of depth and complexity.

David Greenberg, also a trained jazz saxophonist, says the research could have implications for the music industry. ‘A lot of money is put into algorithms to choose what music you may want to listen to, for example on internet music services like Spotify and Apple Music. By knowing an individual’s thinking style, such services might in future be able to fine tune their music recommendations to an individual.’

Dr Jason Rentfrow, the senior author on the study, said that the line of research was significant. It highlighted how music mirrors the self, and can be an expression of how we’re feeling, and is a reflection of our social and cognitive selves. Professor Simon Baron-Cohen, a member of the Cambridge team, added: ‘This new study is a fascinating extension to the ‘empathizing-systemizing’ theory of psychological individual differences. It took a talented PhD student and musician to even think to ask this question. The research may help us to understand those at the extremes, such as people with autism, who are strong systemizers and may experience difficulty socializing.’
