A polar cruise in Greenland

A Delivered over the ship’s microphone at 7.45 p.m. was an invitation few could refuse: ‘There’s a lovely group of walruses on the beach, waiting for us to come, so everyone should get over there.’ This led to some 60 of us, staff as well as passengers of the ship, the Polar Pioneer, landing on the island of Spitsbergen. The walruses were still several hundred metres away from us, but the air was so clear that we could see the shiny white of their tusks. Following a briefing from our expedition leader, Gary Miller (‘By law, we can get no closer than 30 metres’), we advanced slowly towards them. About 35 walruses were asleep on the sand, lying any old way over the top of one another. Through binoculars, we watched as heavy breathing from the mouth of one lifted the tail of another. In a little bay nearby, a few old males swam backwards and forwards between sandy beach and snowy peaks, for so long that even the keenest photographer could take all the photos he could wish for.

B We had got on the ship in Iceland to sail up the coast of Greenland and on to Spitsbergen. We had been drawn by the promise of ‘fantastic icebergs and a fairytale landscape’ — and, above all, by the possibility of seeing polar bears. We got our first and only sighting of a bear on our first day aboard the ship. A group of us were on deck when Gary saw it through binoculars: ‘Look. Walking along a ridge. There’s a little hut on top and it’s to the left of that.’ Some other people caught sight of it, and then Gary was encouraging everyone to board the inflatable boats for a look closer to land. That’s the trouble with polar bears: when you know there’s one about, you also know it’s not safe to make a landing. We cruised up and down for a while, but the bear didn’t reappear. We saw plenty of other wildlife on the trip. If we had been told on Day 1, however, that that would be our only polar bear sighting, it might have made some people question whether the trip would be value for money.

C However, by the end of our two weeks there wasn’t a passenger among us who felt at all let down. Quite the opposite. The Arctic is a place that causes us to become children again. It’s a place where you re-examine what you thought you knew. I used to think I was pretty good at estimating distances. On one outing Gary was driving our boat towards a glacier. It seemed to me about 500 metres off. How far away is it, I asked. ‘Oh, about seven kilometres,’ he answered. The Arctic is a place, too, where you throw yourself out of bed at 1.45 a.m. because you’ve heard other people go by your cabin and you’re frightened you might be missing something. I climbed on deck one day to find most of my fellow-passengers already there, binoculars trained on the ice. What drew us was possibility. ‘You might,’ said Gary, ‘see bear prints or marks showing where a seal has pulled itself up.’ The thing which kept us there, hour after hour, when PPB (possible polar bear) after PPB had turned to nothing, as the pink early morning sky, the brief sighting of a whale, the flight of a bird over the ice, and the quietly hypnotic movement of the ice, the pieces banging inwards and then outwards as if an impatient giant were trying to assemble a badly designed jigsaw puzzle.

D One day in eastern Greenland, we came back from a walk to find we’d missed a drama. A big iceberg in the bay had split in two, resulting in a huge wave that covered our moored boats and one photographer’s backpack. Ann, the ship’s doctor, had been filming the berg as it rocked backwards and forwards, but hadn’t expected it to lose any ice. She showed me the video she’d shot, the last of it upside down as she started running in response to Gary’s shout of ‘Wow! Get the gear!’

E No one minded being out of mobile-phone range. We had a connection, briefly, during our day at the only inhabited place where we landed, Ittoqqortoormiit (population: 450 East Greenlanders and 150 sledge dogs), where the local minister gave us a graphic illustration of global warming. The frozen earth under his church is cracking and, as a result, the building is doing the same, leaning to one side like a ship in a storm. Global warming and its effects came up a few times in the talks with which the staff filled the days spent at sea, but there was no preaching about it. It was as if the speakers believed that showing us the wonders of the Arctic, and what was worth conserving there, would turn us all into passionate conservationists. They were probably right.

F On our last day aboard, with a glacier booming and cracking all round, 16 of my fellow passengers, decided to take ‘the polar plunge’. One after another, they jumped into the sea, while between their jumps Gary, standing in a boat below, pushed lumps of ice away from the ship. And three of them even did it with no clothes on.
