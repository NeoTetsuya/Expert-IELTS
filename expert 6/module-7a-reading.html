Tourism in the Developing World

Although it is often underestimated, the tourism industry has the potential to meet the needs of many people who live in the poorest parts of the world. To start with, tourism is the fourth largest industry in the global economy, generating around $1 trillion, and this money has a direct positive impact on locals. Secondly, tourism itself is changing. When tourism emerged in the 1950s, it was the European countries which benefitted most. Just fifteen nations received 98 percent of all international arrivals, but by 2007, budgets had increased and that number had fallen to 57 percent. In fact, tourism is a major source of income for 83 percent of developing countries, which have become popular destinations, and one-third of the world’s poorest nations depend on it.

The economic power of tourism has transformed many societies in recent years. Compared with other major global industries such as the fuel industry, and chemical and car manufacturing, tourism often has more benefits for the people who live in the nation. There are several reasons for this. To start with, all money which is spent usually stays at the point of production, in other words, in local bars and restaurants in the resort. There are often few foreign middle men, so locally based hotels, restaurants, growers and producers directly receive the income which is spent in the area, and that in turn allows more of the cash to stay within the region.

A second reason is that many countries that lack the raw materials needed to enter profitable industries like the fuel industry or agriculture, are nevertheless able to participate successfully in tourism if they have distinct history and traditions. Such countries can use their unique characteristics to create income and give them an advantage when previously they might not have been able to compete.

The third reason is that tourists often have a broad list of requirements. As well as a variety of hotels and restaurants, modern tourists also look for adventure sports and leisure choices. This enables tourist centres to form complex and varied supply chains of goods and services, which in turn means that there is a demand for people to train in a very wide range of skills, from security to cleaners, art historians to hotel managers and car rental companies.

Many tourism jobs are also often seasonal, and whereas in other parts of the world this has been seen as a problem, in many developing countries it is a bonus as it allows workers to take on jobs in tourism alongside their existing occupation, such as farming.

A final, but perhaps most important benefit that tourism has over other industries is that it encourages the development of facilities which everyone can use, and which without doubt benefit the host nation. Improved healthcare, roads, sports centres and high-end restaurants can be funded by foreign investment but enjoyed by locals and visitors alike, and domestic tourism often also improves as a result. This helps to raise living standards for all.

Because of the benefits, the United Nations has identified the development of tourism as one of the methods poorer countries might use to meet half of the eight Millennium Development Goals (MDGs). For the first goal of reducing poverty, MDG1, the benefits of tourism are obvious. It can provide jobs and creates income for communities that often lack any other possibilities for employment. Nigeria is one country that the United Nations has identified as having huge possibilities from tourism.

The fact that the tourist industry also tends to produce flexible labour markets with a range of jobs can also help to achieve another goal, MDG3, that of promoting gender equality. In Mali, an organisation which trains female artists of the Djenne, one of Mali’s oldest and most visited towns, has had success. In Costa Rica, the female arts cooperatives creating products for the tourism industry have grown considerably and provide many of the workers for the first time with both independent incomes and greater self-confidence.

If carefully managed, tourism can also be an important part of promoting a sustainable environment, meeting the needs of another goal, MDG7. In fact, it goes further than that. Not only is there the possibility to protect the natural environment, but also the historical, archaeological and religious sites too. Areas with strong tourism also have a stronger practice of traditions, arts and crafts. Finally, as tourism by definition involves the transfer of people, cultures and ideas, it is ideally placed to foster effective global partnerships, the last goal, MDG8.
