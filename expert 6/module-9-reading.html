Public health reform in Britain: the 18th and 19th centuries

Nineteenth-century movements to improve public health occurred simultaneously in several European countries. They were built upon foundations which were laid in the period between 1750 and 1830.

From about 1750, the population of Europe increased rapidly, and this resulted in a heightened awareness of the large numbers of infants who were dying, and of how bad conditions in prisons and in mental institutions were. The sharp rise in numbers of people also resulted in the rapid growth of hospitals. Hospitals established in Britain, as the result of voluntary efforts by private citizens, helped to create a pattern that was to become familiar in public health services. First, a social evil is recognised, and studies are undertaken through individual initiative. These efforts shape public opinion and attract governmental attention. Finally, such campaigning leads to government action.

The Industrial Revolution also gave rise to conditions which had a big impact on public health, and focused attention on the problems. The Industrial Revolution and its negative effects were first experienced in England, where the health and welfare of workers deteriorated as industrialisation progressed. Between 1801 and 1841 the population of London doubled, and that of Leeds nearly tripled. With such growth there also came rising death rates. Between 1831 and 1844 the death rate per thousand increased in Birmingham from 14.6 to 27.2; in Bristol, from 16.9 to 31; and in Liverpool, from 21 to 34.8. These figures were the result of an increase in the urban population that far exceeded available housing, and of the subsequent development of conditions that led to widespread disease and poor health. As a result, a movement toward sanitary reform arose which finally led to the establishment of public health institutions.

This era was also characterised by the efforts of doctors to educate people in health matters. For example, James Lind, who had worked as a surgeon in the British Navy, published an article about scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of Vitamin C. Later, British physician Sir John Pringle published a book that discussed ventilation in barracks and the provision of toilets. Two years earlier he had written about prison fever (now thought to be typhus), and again he emphasised the same needs, as well as personal hygiene.

Humanitarians and philanthropists in Britain also worked to educate the people and the government on problems associated with population growth, poverty, and epidemics. In 1781, the philosopher Jeremy Bentham proposed the idea that the greatest good of the greatest number of people should be the standard against which the morality of certain actions might be measured. In 1798 English economist and demographer Thomas Malthus wrote about population growth, its dependence on a steady supply of food, and the control of breeding. British physician Thomas Southwood Smith founded the Health of Towns Association in 1839, and by 1848 he was serving as a member of the new government department, then called the General Board of Health. In this position he published reports on quarantine, cholera, yellow fever, and the benefits of sanitary improvements.

The Poor Law Commission, created by the government in 1834, explored problems of community health and suggested means for solving them. Its report, in 1838, argued that the expenditure necessary to adopt and maintain preventative measures would ultimately amount to less than the cost of dealing with the diseases themselves. Sanitary surveys proved that a relationship exists between communicable disease and dirt in the environment, and it was suggested that safeguarding public health was the province of engineers rather than physicians.

The Public Health Act of 1848 established a General Board of Health to provide guidance and aid in sanitary matters for local authorities, whose earlier efforts had been impeded by lack of a central authority. The national board had authority to establish local boards of health, and to investigate sanitary conditions in particular districts. Since that time several public health acts have been passed to regulate a whole range of activities, such as sewage and refuse disposal, the housing of animals, the water supply, prevention and control of disease, registration and inspection of private nursing homes and hospitals, the notification of births, and the provision of maternity and child welfare services.
