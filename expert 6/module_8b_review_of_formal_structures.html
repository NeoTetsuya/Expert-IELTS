Review of Formal Structures

Elevate your academic writing! Master formal grammatical structures including the passive voice, complex sentences, and academic "that" clauses.

The Passive Voice in Academic Writing

In formal research and reports, we use the passive voice to highlight the action or object rather than the person performing it.

Active Focus (Informal) 👤

Focus on the Agent

Emphasizes who performed the action (shipping company, fishermen, researcher).

"The shipping company ships the goods worldwide."

"Fishermen take the fish from the sea in a net."

Passive Focus (Academic) ⚙️

Focus on Action / Process

Omits or de-emphasizes the agent to maintain objective academic tone.

"The goods are shipped to the other side of the world."

"The fish are taken from the sea in a net."

Interactive Focus Switcher

Active vs. Academic Passive

Active Voice (Agent Focused)

"The company ships the goods across the world."

Subject = "The company". Focuses on who does the action.

Academic Passive Voice (Process Focused)

"The goods are shipped to the other side of the world."

Subject = "The goods". Agent ("by the company") is omitted as unnecessary.

💡

Academic Guardrail

Use the passive voice when the process, result, or object of study is more important than the individual researcher or worker!

Complex Sentences & Clause Structures

A complex sentence combines one independent clause with one or more dependent clauses.

Anatomy of a Complex Sentence

Independent + Dependent
"Although they were planted, the weather caused the crops to die."
⚠️ Dependent Clause (Incomplete): "Although they were planted" — Starts with subordinating conjunction although. Makes no sense on its own.
✔️ Independent Clause (Complete): "the weather caused the crops to die." — Contains complete subject + verb. Makes complete sense alone.

3 Ways to Form Complex Academic Sentences

1. Conjunctions

Subordinating Conjunctions

Link clauses using because, although, while, when, unless, even though, as, if.

"Because the drought lasted so long, crops were damaged."

"While the rain fell, farmers harvested crops."

2. Relative Clauses

Defining Relative Clauses

Embed essential identifying detail using who, which, that, where.

"People who grow their own vegetables are increasing in number."

3. Participle Clauses

Reduced Participle Clauses

Reduce clauses using -ing (active) or -ed (passive) participles.

"Those people growing their own food are less dependent."

Academic "That" Clauses

In spoken English, that is often omitted. In formal academic writing, including "that" is expected!

Personal / Direct Claim

believe / think / state

Introduces an author's argument or direct statement.

"I believe that people should do more to fight hunger."
Consensus Passive

It is agreed that...

Expresses general academic agreement or shared findings.

"It is agreed that convenience food affects public health."
Research Conclusion

It can be concluded that...

Summarizes data-driven deductions or research outcomes.

"It can be concluded that cholesterol has varied health impacts."
Spoken vs. Academic Formalizer

Always Include "THAT" in Writing

Informal Spoken English (Omitted 'that')

"I think people should do more to fight hunger."

'That' is missing. Common in everyday speech, but discouraged in formal essays.

Formal Academic Essay (Included 'that')

"I believe that people should do more to fight hunger."

Explicit 'that' clause adds structural clarity and academic tone.
