Three Common Reported Verb Structures

Pattern A

Subject + Verb + that + Clause

Verbs such as deny, claim, admit, state, mention follow this pattern directly without needing an object noun/pronoun.

"She denied that she had changed the date of our meeting."

💡 Verbs: deny, admit, say, claim, state, mention
Pattern B

Subject + Verb + to + Object + Clause/Infinitive

Verbs such as explain, suggest, point out require the preposition to before the listener object.

"The manager explained to me that the offer had expired."

💡 Preposition Alert: Never omit 'to' with explain! (e.g. explained to me)
Pattern C

Subject + Verb + Object + Infinitive/Clause

Verbs such as tell, advise, warn, persuade, encourage take a direct listener object immediately followed by an infinitive or clause.

"Many people told me to go and study abroad."

💡 Verbs: tell, advise, urge, persuade, instruct

⚡ Modals That DO NOT Backshift in Reported Speech

While modal verbs like can ➔ could and will ➔ would backshift, the following modals remain unchanged in reported speech:

should would might could needn't
⭐ Band 8.0+ Academic Stance Evaluative & Argumentative Reporting Verbs
Writing Task 2 Critical Analysis

Band 8+ writers replace generic reporting verbs (said, told, thought) with evaluative verbs that signal precisely how reliable or controversial a claim is:

Strong Assertion

"Proponents contend / maintain that early bilingual education accelerates cognitive development."

Tentative Hypothesis

"Sociologists postulate / hypothesize that urbanization weakens traditional family ties."

Concession / Rebuttal

"While economists concede that inflation has dropped, they refute claims of full recovery."

a. Noun + Relative Clause

Post-modification

Expands a noun by appending a relative clause (which, that, who) to specify exact traits or locations.

"Houses which are in low land are more susceptible to flooding."

b. Adjective + Noun

Pre-modification

Adds qualitative descriptive adjectives directly before the head noun.

Prominent positions are held by a range of people in business and politics.

c. Noun + Noun (Compound)

Noun Modifier

Uses a primary noun as a modifying adjective before another noun to specify professional roles or categories.

A database designer is a role sought after by many companies.

d. Noun + Prepositional Phrase

Prepositional Post-mod

Appends a prepositional phrase (in, of, with, on) directly after the noun to denote scope or context.

The people in the study were asked several questions.

2. Noun Phrases for Quantities & Data (IELTS Task 1 & 2)

Essential structures for summarizing statistics, survey demographics, and proportions smoothly in academic writing:

Fraction Pattern
A (fraction) + of + (category)

"A fifth of homeowners wanted to move house."

→ Fraction 'a fifth' (20%) + of + category 'homeowners'.

Majority / Minority Pattern
The/A (majority / minority) + of + (category)

"The majority of young adults wish to work overseas."

→ Proportion 'the majority' + of + category 'young adults'.

⭐ Band 8.0+ Lexical Density Academic Nominalization & Packaging Ideas
Task 1 & Task 2 Density

Nominalization transforms verbal clauses into compact noun phrases. This increases informational density and removes informal conversational tone:

Conversational Clause (Low Band)

"Because the government invested heavily in public healthcare, mortality rates fell."

Nominalized Compound (Band 8.0+)

"Substantial government investment in public healthcare precipitated a marked reduction in mortality rates."

Conversational Clause (Low Band)

"When individuals consume too much fast food, they tend to become obese."

Nominalized Compound (Band 8.0+)

"Excessive fast food consumption is closely correlated with the onset of obesity."
